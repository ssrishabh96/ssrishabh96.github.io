PUBLIC COMMENT OPPOSING THE PROPOSED $103,265 H-1B PETITION FEE

A forward-looking immigration policy must serve the long-term national interest

Submitted by Rishabh Agrawal on September 24, 2026

To U.S. Citizenship and Immigration Services:

I submit this comment as an H-1B professional who earned a graduate degree in the United States and contributes to U.S. technology and infrastructure. I oppose the Department of Homeland Security’s proposal to impose an additional $103,265 fee on every cap-subject H-1B petition, including petitions for beneficiaries eligible for the U.S. advanced-degree allocation.

Requested action. DHS should withdraw the proposed rule. The fee is disproportionate, insufficiently connected to the service for which it would be charged, economically exclusionary, and contrary to the long-term national interest of the United States.

America advances by competing for talent, not pricing it out

The proposal points backward. America became the world’s leading center of science, engineering, medicine, and enterprise by attracting capable people and giving them room to build. A six-figure charge on a new skilled-worker petition sends the opposite message: opportunity in the United States is available only when an employer can pay an extraordinary toll before USCIS even adjudicates the petition.

That is not a strategy for national strength. It is a barrier that will predictably favor the largest and richest institutions while narrowing access for startups, smaller businesses, regional employers, and emerging industries. It will also fall directly on the pathway used by many graduates of U.S. universities, including those covered by the advanced-degree allocation. The United States invests in educating these graduates and then, under this proposal, would impose an additional $103,265 obstacle on employers seeking to retain them.

A serious national-interest analysis must ask more than whether some employers will continue to file petitions. It must ask which employers will be excluded, which projects will be delayed or moved abroad, which graduates will leave, and which competitors will benefit. The Notice of Proposed Rulemaking (NPRM) does not adequately answer those questions.

The proposal is an unprecedented cross-subsidy, not a service-based fee

The proposal is not calibrated to the cost of adjudicating an H-1B petition. DHS states that the fee would finance broad immigration-related operations across USCIS, U.S. Immigration and Customs Enforcement, U.S. Customs and Border Protection, the Executive Office for Immigration Review, the Department of State, and the Department of Labor. The NPRM’s proposed allocation sends only 34.2 percent of the projected revenue to USCIS, while 33.7 percent would go to EOIR, 11.9 percent to ICE, and the remainder to CBP, DOS, and DOL.

DHS itself acknowledges that it has not historically imposed a fee on one specific immigration subpopulation to fund general operating costs across USCIS, other DHS components, and other executive-branch departments. That novelty matters. Calling this charge a filing fee does not resolve the central policy problem: a narrow group of employers would be made responsible for billions of dollars in costs far beyond the adjudication of their petitions and, in many instances, unrelated to any direct service they receive.

The agency’s ability-to-pay rationale is also unpersuasive. An employer’s legal obligation to pay a skilled worker a salary is not proof that the employer has an extra $103,265 available for a government charge. Salary buys years of productive labor; this fee buys no additional adjudicative service and remains due at filing, with no guarantee of approval. Treating payroll obligations as evidence of spare financial capacity confuses the cost of employing a person with the capacity to absorb an unrelated levy.

DHS quantifies the burden but not the claimed public benefit

The agency’s own regulatory analysis presents a stark imbalance. DHS projects approximately $8.78 billion in annual costs to cap-subject petitioners and estimates ten-year discounted costs of approximately $74.9 billion at a three-percent discount rate. Yet the NPRM reports monetized benefits as “Not Estimated” and lists effects on wages and economic growth as “N/A.” A rule of this scale should not be finalized on the basis of asserted indirect benefits that the agency has not quantified, weighed, or tested against the rule’s substantial costs.

The small-business findings are particularly troubling. DHS estimates that the proposal would have a significant economic impact on 11,051 small entities—76 percent of the small entities it identified as affected. A uniform fee does not create equal treatment when the capacity to pay is radically unequal. It entrenches incumbent advantage and converts access to talent into a function of organizational wealth.

The proposal therefore risks producing the opposite of economic progress. Large firms may continue to participate while smaller competitors withdraw. That outcome would reduce competition for talent, weaken startup formation, and concentrate opportunity. The agency should not equate continued aggregate demand with the absence of economic harm.

Aggregate demand cannot justify exclusion by wealth

DHS relies in part on a 2026 working paper to suggest that demand for H-1B visas would remain above the statutory allocation even with a fee above $100,000. That inference is too narrow for a national policy decision. Demand remaining above 85,000 says nothing about the composition of that demand, the employers priced out, the loss of competition, or the relocation of work outside the United States. A market can remain oversubscribed while becoming less innovative, less open, and less beneficial to the country.

The NPRM also states that USCIS historically selects more than 85,000 registrations because some petitions are denied, rejected, or withdrawn, but then assumes only 85,000 fee-paying petitions under the proposed rule. That assumption risks leaving statutory numbers unused if ordinary attrition continues. At minimum, DHS should not finalize a massive charge while it acknowledges that it is still studying current-year petitioner behavior.

Legitimate concerns require targeted enforcement, not a blanket barrier

Protecting U.S. workers is a legitimate and necessary objective. So are preventing fraud, enforcing wage rules, and ensuring that the H-1B program is used for genuine specialty-occupation needs. But a uniform $103,265 charge does not distinguish between an abusive employer and a compliant one, between labor arbitrage and a genuine skills shortage, or between a multinational corporation and a young company trying to hire one specialized engineer.

If DHS has evidence of fraud or wage suppression, it should address that conduct directly: enforce prevailing-wage requirements, investigate repeat violators, improve audit targeting, strengthen transparency, and penalize proven abuse. Targeted enforcement advances fairness without discarding the contribution of lawful, high-skilled immigration. A blunt financial barrier is not a substitute for administration.

Public concern deserves consideration; prejudice does not deserve a veto

The notice-and-comment process is essential because it permits the public to provide evidence, identify consequences, and test an agency’s reasoning. But it is not a plebiscite in which the loudest fear should determine policy. Government has a duty to distinguish evidence-based concern from resentment toward immigrants and from racially charged or xenophobic sentiment. Such sentiment will always exist in a free society; the answer is not to give it controlling weight over the country’s future.

America has repeatedly faced the choice between retreat and progress. Progress requires moral clarity: people should be judged by their conduct, skills, and contribution—not treated as a social threat because they were born elsewhere. The federal government can listen respectfully to public concern without converting fear of outsiders into an indiscriminate economic barrier.

Immigration policy will inevitably be debated in politics, but the national interest cannot be reduced to short-term political incentives. USCIS and DHS should administer the law through evidence, proportionality, and institutional judgment. The question is not which message produces the quickest applause. The question is which policy makes the United States stronger ten, twenty, and fifty years from now.

DHS should withdraw the rule and pursue narrower alternatives

DHS should withdraw the proposal. If the Department concludes that additional funding is necessary, it should publish a transparent, service-based cost allocation and consider alternatives that do not make one narrow class of employers finance broad government operations. At a minimum, any future proposal should:

  1. Tie petition fees to the actual cost of adjudication, fraud prevention, and program administration associated with the petition or program.
  2. Use congressional appropriations—not an immigration-status-based levy—to fund government-wide enforcement, immigration-court, and other public functions whose benefits are national and diffuse.
  3. Target proven abuse through audits, wage enforcement, debarment, and penalties rather than presuming that every cap-subject petition creates the same risk.
  4. Protect competition by providing meaningful relief for small businesses and startups, with anti-avoidance rules instead of rejecting relief merely because it requires careful administration.
  5. Evaluate effects on innovation, offshoring, regional employers, U.S.-educated graduates, wages, growth, and the likelihood that the annual cap will be fully used.
  6. Reopen the comment period after publishing complete current-year demand evidence and a quantified comparison of reasonable alternatives.

America should move forward

America’s national interest must come first. But the national interest is served by confidence, merit, competition, and progress—not by retreating from the world’s talent or allowing the most fearful voices to set the horizon of public policy. A nation as strong as the United States should be capable of protecting workers, enforcing its laws, and welcoming needed skill at the same time.

The proposed fee would move the country backward. It would ration opportunity by wealth, weaken smaller American employers, burden U.S.-educated talent, and finance broad governmental functions through a narrow and poorly matched charge. I respectfully urge DHS and USCIS to withdraw the proposed rule and replace it, if necessary, with a transparent, proportionate, evidence-based approach that advances the country rather than closing it off.

America should always move forward.

Respectfully submitted,

Rishabh Agrawal
